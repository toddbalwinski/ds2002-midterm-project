1. Data Quality Impact
Describe a specific data quality issue you encountered in the supplied data. How did your cleaning decision change the outcome of your analysis? What would have happened if you had not caught it?
Russell -The main problem, then, was to find that there were four different SKUs for Strawberry Pop-Tarts, namely: PT-12, SKU#459812, POPTART-STRAW and PT_STRAWBERRY. If we had not combined them, the highest SKU would have ranked 3rd out of 45 SKU's during the surge week, and that SKU would have carried only about 27% of the real demand. If we combine them, then Pop-Tarts is the no. 1 out of 20 products. The real demand increased from 73 units/day (Sep 1 to 15, baseline) to 377 units/day in the surge week (Sep 19 to 25) and reached a maximum of 678 units/day on Sep 25, which is 9.3 times of the baseline demand. The fragmented view would show four items selling between 90 and 103 units per day, each of them in the middle of the pack in terms of demand in the surge, thus leading us to stock only a fourth of the required inventory for those four items. Consolidation only changed the labels, the total units, 49,584 units, stayed the same.
Todd - The different SKU codes definitely mattered the most as we cut down from 45 codes to just 20 real products. We made every decision of what to combine so that was probably the most impactful thing that we did and if we made human error in the process, either missing a combination or joining two different items, then there was nothing to catch it but ourselves. This changed our analysis as products that were not too impactful before became big players in the numbers after the combination. If we had not caught this then we would have had different leading products and jumping markers.
2. ETL Trade-offs
You made choices about how to standardize SKUs, handle missing timestamps, and join weather data. Pick one decision and explain: what alternative approach could you have taken, and how might it have changed your results?
Todd - One choice we made was aligning the weather and sales data times to make sure the UTC to ETC difference didn't distort our daily totals. We could have skipped this step and instead just grouped the data without converting but that would have shifted some evening shopping to the next day. This is impactful as it would have shifted our lag analysis and make shoppers look less forward thinking than we found them to truly be.
Russell - The decision I’d point to is how we parsed timestamps. Our first draft used pandas format=”mixed”, which guesses the format row by row. We replaced it with five explicit formats and an assert that fails if anything is left unparsed. The reason is that 7,440 rows use numeric dates like 09/05/2024, where month and day could be swapped. If those had been read day-first, 1,920 of them would have landed on the wrong date all outside September, and every baseline and surge average would have shifted without any error message. When we compared the two approaches on our own data they gave identical dates, so mixed would have been fine here. But that depends on the pandas version, and we’d rather have a pipeline that fails loudly than one that happens to work. We’d make the same call again. The cost was a few more lines of code and having to look at every format in the file.
3. Pipeline Trust
The Walmart CDO argued that the biggest risk is not the model — it is the pipeline. Based on your experience in this project, do you agree or disagree? What was the most fragile part of your data pipeline?
Russell - I agree with the CDO and our project backs it up. The modelling part of this project was just a few averages and correlations. The pipeline was the place things could quietly go wrong: 60 spellings of store_id for 12 stores, 37 spellings of category, five timestamp formats, 884 transactions without timestamp at all, two different ways to store dates in database (218 rows look like 20240912). Dates were the most fragile part. A wrong date doesn't crash anything, but shifts all windows. The second weak spot was the combination of the two sources. The database's Entertainment units run about 5.7 times the point-of-sale file (ranging from 3.9 to 7.2 depending on the day), so the two don't reconcile in level. The inventory table only samples 12 SKUs per store per day, and 355 of its rows have SKU codes that are not in the catalogue. What helped the most was writing the checks as assertions into the notebook (30 duplicates removed, 12 stores, 30 days, every SKU mapped) and maintaining a decision log using row counts.
Todd - I agree with the CDO as you can tell a right or wrong story with good data, but you cant tell anything but a wrong story with wrong data, and that is what a bad pipeline produces. It is also the part that is not forward facing so it is under much less scrutiny during normal presenting circumstances. One of the most fragile parts of our database was that some rows stored their dates without any separating dashes which when using pd.to_datetime with coercing errors on, turns them blank with no notifications.

4. Business vs. Data
For Question 5 (board games), your team had to make a recommendation with imperfect data. How did you weigh statistical evidence against business risk? Would you have decided differently with more time or more data?
Russell - The +250% on the brief is basically the busiest day (Sep 25). Over the seven days before it made landfall, Entertainment went up about 125% in transactions and 145% in database. We do not think it is an artifact: it is comprised of only two items - Monopoly and Playing Cards, it had more than double the amount of transactions per day and still the amount of items in a transaction stayed the same, so people did go in more. Board games did not rise faster than the store as a whole in the transactions and the data in the inventory was only a sample. So we recommended stocking extra, sized to general storm lift or about 2x to 2.5x, not 250%. Board games do not go off, so overstocking costs little compared with a lost sale. With more time, we would want a second storm and full daily inventory.
Todd - we trusted the transaction level data from the POS and looked into the split between board games and playing cards. AS for the business we looked into the cost of a mistake in our recommendation. Stocking for a 250% increase takes up truck space and storage capacity from emergency items and foods that are much more important during a storm. So for that reason we decided it best to not stock in accordance with the increase but instead only with our general storm lift of two times, as being wrong would hurt people far more than if we were right and they got some more entertainment. With more data we could see if board games lift around each hurricane and in that case stock more as the demand is clear and we can make more sales and give people something to do while trapped at home.
